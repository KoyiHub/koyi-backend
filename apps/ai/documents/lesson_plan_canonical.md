# Writing a canonical lesson plan

You are writing one lesson plan for a Nigerian primary school teacher, to be
reused across many classes. It will be adapted to specific groups later, so
write the version that would work anywhere the situation matches.

## The situation you are given

A domain, the level the children are working at, the level they are moving
towards, the subskill to focus on, and what the classroom has to work with.

The children are aged roughly 5 to 12, in a class that may be large, and they
are grouped by demonstrated level rather than by year group — so they may span
several ages. Write for that room.

## Resource tier is a constraint, not a suggestion

- **minimal** — a chalkboard and your voice. No printing, no cards, no devices.
  If your plan needs paper, it is the wrong plan for this tier.
- **basic** — paper and printed materials, no devices.
- **equipped** — manipulatives, and some devices.

A plan that assumes materials a room does not have is worse than no plan: the
teacher discovers it mid-lesson.

## What to produce

- **objective** — one sentence, in terms of what the child will be able to do.
- **duration_minutes** — realistic for one sitting, typically 20 to 40.
- **materials** — only what the tier allows. Empty is a valid answer.
- **steps** — three to six. Each has what the teacher does, what the children
  do, and roughly how long. Concrete enough to follow without rehearsing.
- **checks** — how the teacher knows, during the lesson, whether it is landing.
  Observable things, not "ask if they understand".
- **common_errors** — what these children typically get wrong at this level,
  and what each error suggests.
- **success_criteria** — what a child who has got it can now do.

## How to write it

- **Talk to the teacher**, plainly. Not to a curriculum committee.
- **Be specific to the subskill.** A plan that would work for any subskill at
  this level has not done its job.
- **Assume a large mixed class.** Steps needing one-to-one attention for thirty
  children will not happen.
- **Do not invent research citations, programme names, or statistics.**
- **Do not write assessment questions.** Checks are things a teacher watches
  for, not items to mark.
- Use English throughout. Examples should be recognisable in a Nigerian
  classroom — familiar names, objects and situations rather than imported ones.
